What DTC Brands Get Wrong About Fulfillment SLAs

March 23, 2026 4 min read By the MFS Team

Most DTC brands treat fulfillment SLAs as a formality. That's a mistake. Here's what the fine print actually means — and what you should push for.

The SLA Isn't Just a Piece of Paper

A service level agreement defines what your 3PL is actually promising you. Turnaround times, accuracy standards, error liability, support response windows — it's all in there.

The problem is most founders skim it. They're focused on pricing and onboarding, and the SLA feels like legal boilerplate. By the time something goes wrong, they realize the agreement they signed doesn't actually protect them.

The Most Misunderstood SLA Metric: Fulfillment Speed

Here's where brands get caught. A 3PL says they offer "same-day or next-day fulfillment." That sounds great. But read the clause carefully — most of those commitments apply only to orders received before a specific cutoff time, only on business days, and only when inventory is properly checked in.

If your inventory arrives on a Thursday afternoon and the cutoff is noon, you're already looking at Monday before orders ship. That's not next-day anything.

What to negotiate instead: a defined cutoff time (we use 2 PM EST), a guaranteed processing window after check-in, and explicit language about holiday and weekend handling.

Order Accuracy Clauses Are Often Toothless

Most 3PL contracts include an accuracy guarantee — something like "99% order accuracy." Looks strong on paper. But dig into how that's calculated and what the remedy actually is.

Some 3PLs calculate accuracy as a monthly aggregate across all clients, not against your specific order volume. Others only offer a re-ship credit if you file a formal claim within 48 hours with photographic proof. Real-world error resolution rarely works that cleanly.

The number to benchmark against: industry-standard accuracy sits around 98-99%. At MFS, we operate at 99.9% — and our SLA defines accuracy at the individual order level, not a blended rate. That distinction matters when you're shipping 10,000 units a month.

What Happens When They Miss the SLA?

This is the clause most founders never read — and regret later. The SLA defines the remedy when your 3PL drops the ball. Often that remedy is a service credit, capped at a percentage of your monthly invoice.

That doesn't cover the cost of a customer who churns. It doesn't cover a refund you issued because an order arrived late. It doesn't cover the one-star review that's now sitting on your Shopify product page.

Negotiate for specific, proportional remedies tied to the actual impact — not a blanket credit that barely covers a rounding error on your invoice.

The Support SLA Nobody Talks About

Fulfillment errors are time-sensitive. A mispicked order, a lost shipment, a duplicate charge — these need to be resolved in hours, not days.

Most enterprise 3PLs route support through a ticketing system. Their SLA says they'll respond within 24-48 business hours. That's useless when an influencer just posted about your product and 2,000 orders came in with a pricing error.

Ask directly: who do I call when something is on fire? Get a name, a phone number, and a defined response window in writing. If a 3PL can't answer that question clearly, that tells you everything.

What to Actually Negotiate Before You Sign

Here's a short checklist of what to push for in any fulfillment SLA:

  • Cutoff time — specific, in writing, with clarity on weekends and holidays
  • Inventory check-in window — how long before received inventory is pick-ready
  • Accuracy definition — per-order, not blended across your account or client base
  • Error remedy — tied to actual impact, not just invoice credits
  • Support escalation path — a real human, a real number, a real response window
  • Carve-outs and exclusions — understand exactly what voids their commitments (carrier delays, weather, peak volume surcharges)

The Takeaway

A fulfillment SLA is only as good as the specificity behind it. Vague language protects the 3PL, not your brand.

Before you sign anything, pressure-test every metric. Ask how it's measured, what the remedy is, and what the exceptions are. A good 3PL partner won't flinch at those questions — they'll welcome them.
